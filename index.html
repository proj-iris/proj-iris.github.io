IRIS

A low-power, event-driven AI system-on-chip for edge computing

Many edge devices that need AI, such as monitoring cameras, robots and people or animal detectors, run on little power and can't send their data to the cloud. IRIS builds an AI chip that works with an event camera: the camera reports only the pixels that change, and the chip computes only when those changes arrive.

The project is a joint effort of the University of Aizu (Japan) and the Institute of System Integration, Le Quy Don Technical University (Vietnam). It runs from January 2027 to June 2030 and also trains early-career researchers in low-power chip design.

< 1 WASIC power target
50 fpsreal time at 50 MHz
80–90%energy saving compared with an ANN on RGB frames

Approach

  • Event sensing. An event camera only reports changes, so a typical scene produces far less data than a normal camera.
  • Spiking neural network accelerator. A dedicated event-driven accelerator processes only the incoming spikes and exploits sparsity in space and time.
  • Approximate clock-gating. Inactive circuit blocks are switched off, and some clock events are skipped even when input is present, trading a small loss of accuracy for a large saving in energy.
  • From model to silicon. The design goes from AI models and RTL through FPGA prototypes to ASIC tapeouts and a system deployed in real applications.

Why IRIS

The iris is the part of the eye that controls how much light comes in, and an event camera is an eye that sees only what changes. The logo is an eye drawn in dots, like spikes in a spiking neural network.

Iris is also the Japanese iris flower, Iris ensata, a nod to the project's home in Japan and the source of the purple colours.

In hanakotoba, the Japanese language of flowers, the iris (花菖蒲, hanashōbu) stands for “good news” (うれしい知らせ) and “I believe in you” (あなたを信じる). Good news fits a chip that wakes only when a message arrives, and trust fits a partnership between Japan and Vietnam.
